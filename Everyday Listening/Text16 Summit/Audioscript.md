20 heads of government will fly into Vladivostok's new international airport, built to handle 15 million tourists, 10 times last year's passenger load. They will zoom down brand-new highways and cross a new billion-dollar bridge to a brand-new American-style university campus. This weekend, it hosts Pacific Rim national leaders; next month, it will host 25,000 students. Artyom Lukin, a director of international research at the Far Eastern Federal University, calls the summit a coming out party for Russia as a modern Pacific power. "If you want to be a great power in the Pacific, you need to have a developed Pacific coast, which is the Russian Far East." Vladivostok means "to rule the East." Here on Russia Island on the Sea of Japan, the Kremlin has built this bridge and this university campus to open Russia's doors to Asia. Russia's goal is to double its trade with Asia-Pacific partners. Economic development minister Andrei Belousov talks of the Kremlin's commitment. Two years ago, this was an empty field. There were no roads, there was not that airport terminal where you arrived, there were not the bridges, there were not the buildings where we are now. 1 million square meters were constructed. Vladivostok's upgrade is so abrupt that a cruise ship had to be floated in to house 500 summit participants, and work is underway on the city's first two international chain hotels, both Hyatts. Also under construction is a new opera and ballet theater. Work starts soon on an oil refinery, a liquefied natural gas plant, and a massive gambling zone for Asian tourists. The goal is to make Vladivostok attractive for young people. Professor Lukin says one-third of his students usually leave the Russian Far East for jobs elsewhere. From repelling Asian invaders to attracting Asian investors, Vladivostok now takes on a brand-new role.
